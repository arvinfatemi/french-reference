So the confusable four sort themselves out: je parlai 📚 (p. simple) · je parlais (imparfait) · je parlerai (futur) · je parlerais (conditionnel). Hear an /r/ before the ending → future family. Ending in -s → the “was/would” family.

How verbs actually sort — read this first

why neither “3 groups” nor “-er/-ir/-re” is the real map
The two systems you'll see in textbooks both leak. Endings (-er/-ir/-re) sort by infinitive spelling — but finir and partir both end in -ir and conjugate nothing alike. The three groups sort by behavior — but the 3e groupe is a junk drawer: avoir and pouvoir land in it together while sharing only the label “irregular”. The honest axis isn't the four letters at the end of the infinitive — it's what the stem does in the plural. Sort by that:

The takeaway: the three model verbs below (parler / finir / vendre) are the three regular cores — not three exhaustive “groups”. Everything else is an irregular you learn one at a time, and the irregular banks throughout this doc are where they live. Don't try to derive avoir from a rule; there isn't one.

Inside the loose families — sort by the plural mutation, not the spelling

the real unit of categorization
Every “loose” verb runs the same mechanical move: a short singular stem (+ s·s·t) and a longer plural stem. The only question is which consonant appears in the plural — and that, not the infinitive ending, is the real category. The test for a genuine family: knowing the family + the je form, can you produce nous / ils and the participle? Where the infinitive shape maps 1:1 to one mutation, the spelling is a reliable behavioral label — conjugate a new one on sight. Where it doesn't (-ire), it's only a spelling rhyme.
Proof the ending doesn't decide: servir (-ir) and suivre (-vre) run the identical move — je sers → nous servons, je suis → nous suivons, both participles in -i. Meanwhile rire, which shares the -re ending with suivre, does something else entirely (no consonant at all). Ending predicts nothing; the mutation predicts everything.

Why -ire is the leaky one — identical infinitive shape, different inserted consonant per cluster:
lire, élire → je lis / nous lisons · insert -s- · pp lu dire, suffire → je dis / nous disons · -s- · pp dit (vous dites!) écrire, décrire → j'écris / nous écrivons · insert -v- · pp écrit rire, sourire → je ris / nous rions · nothing inserted · pp ri
rire breaks its own family's rule — the whole point of “loose -RE” is that the plural inserts a consonant, and rire inserts none. Stem is just ri- everywhere, which ironically makes it one of the easiest verbs in French. ⚠️ One trap: imparfait nous riions / subjonctif que nous riions — double-i, because stem ri- + ending -ions.
One move to internalize for all of them: singular = short stem + s · s · t; plural = stem + the inserted consonant + regular endings. Hear it as a rhythm — lis / lisons, écris / écrivons, conduis / conduisons, crains / craignons. The participle still has to be memorized per verb.

Les temps composés — one machine, six tenses

passé composé, plus-que-parfait, futur antérieur, conditionnel passé, subjonctif passé 🗣️ passé composé = spoken core
AVOIR or ÊTRE (conjugated)+PARTICIPE PASSÉ — the tense of the auxiliary is the only thing that changes
Learn the machine once. Every compound tense is just the auxiliary moved into a different simple tense. If you know your simple tenses + the participle, you get six tenses for free.

-u : avoir → euboire → bulire → lu voir → vusavoir → supouvoir → pu devoir → dûvouloir → vouluconnaître → connu venir → venutenir → tenucourir → couru recevoir → reçuvivre → vécupleuvoir → plufalloir → fallu

Which auxiliary? ÊTRE for the 17 movement/state verbs (DR & MRS VANDERTRAMP: Devenir, Revenir, Monter, Rester, Sortir, Venir, Aller, Naître, Descendre, Entrer, Rentrer, Tomber, Retourner, Arriver, Mourir, Partir, Passer) and all pronominal verbs (se lever, s'amuser…). Everything else → AVOIR. ⚠️ monter, descendre, sortir, passer, rentrer, retourner switch to AVOIR when they take a direct object: j'ai sorti la poubelle vs je suis sorti.
Agreement rules (applies to every compound tense):
① ÊTRE → participle agrees with the subject: elle est allée, ils sont partis, elles sont restées.
② AVOIR → no agreement… unless a direct object comes before the verb: j'ai vu la maison → je l'ai vue ; les pommes que j'ai achetées.
③ Pronominal verbs → agree with the reflexive pronoun if it is the direct object: elle s'est lavée, but elle s'est lavé les mains (no agreement — les mains is the direct object and comes after).

A past action that didn't happen — regret, hypotheticals, unconfirmed news: j'aurais aimé venir (I would have liked to come). Auxiliary = the conditionnel you already know (aur- / ser- + imparfait endings), then the participle.

These two are a pair — the “third conditional”: Si + plus-que-parfait → conditionnel passé in the result. Si j'avais su, je ne serais pas venu (if I had known, I wouldn't have come). Compare the three si levels:
① Si + présent → futur: si tu veux, on ira
② Si + imparfait → conditionnel présent: si tu voulais, on irait
③ Si + plus-que-parfait → conditionnel passé: si tu avais voulu, on serait allés
🇨🇦 High-value for TEF: level ② (regret/politeness) and level ③ (counterfactual) carry most persuasion and reflection tasks. j'aurais dû, tu aurais pu, on aurait pensé que… are ready-made chunks.

Where it's obligatory: after quand, dès que, lorsque, après que, une fois que when the first action finishes before the second — and French uses a real future here where English uses the present: quand j'aurai fini, je t'appellerai (when I have finished, I'll call you). Same trap as the futur simple after quand.
Also expresses a guess about the past: il n'est pas là — il aura oublié (he must have forgotten). Parallel to the conditionnel passé's "unconfirmed" use.
